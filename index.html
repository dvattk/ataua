AτA: A Benchmark for Multi‑Agent Interaction in Agentic Economies

When both sides of a market send AI agents, who wins?

We put travellers' agents and airlines' agents in one shared marketplace and score the outcome for both sides. The agents book the trips. The market they build together is far from the best one available.

Each traveller and each airline is represented by its own agent. User agents send requests (solid) and airline agents respond (dashed); seats come out of shared inventory.

Why the gap?

Reading the agents' traces, two patterns explain much of it, one on each side of the market.

Every agent wins its own race

Under scarce supply, each user agent books the best trip for its own customer. Agent A takes the last two economy seats on Delta's August 2 flight. Agent B wanted the same flight for its own party of two, found it sold out, and paid $1,631 for a United trip on other dates.

A coordinated allocation gives B the Delta flight and moves A to flights a day or two later. A gives up a little; the market gains a lot.

Good buyers, poor sellers

Airline agents can hold the fare, concede part of the 30% discount, offer another cabin, or add paid services. Instead they reach for one lever: the full discount, usually in the first quote. Every booking got the maximum 30% off (858 under scarce supply, 531 under abundant supply), while customers spent a median of only 52–55% of their budgets.

Does a pricier airline agent help?

A traveller calls their agent now and then; an airline's agent serves everyone, so its inference bill adds up. We fixed the travellers' model and swapped the airline's model to see what the extra spend buys.

2.05×higher airline inference cost per task under scarce supply than under abundant supply, across matched pairings

Costs are upper reference estimates from recorded tokens and public prices, per five-traveller task. Runs differ in dates and settings, so comparisons are descriptive.
