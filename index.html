AτA

A benchmark for multi-agent interaction in agentic economies.

What happens when travellers and airlines both send AI agents to the market? AτA puts user agents and airline agents in a shared airline marketplace, grounded in real U.S. fare data, and scores the outcome for both sides, not just whether a booking was made.

Why a market, not a single chat?

Benchmarks for agents usually test one agent serving one user: did it book the right flight? But once agents act in real markets, completing the task is not enough. A flight booked for one traveller is a seat another traveller cannot have, and a discount that delights a customer is revenue an airline gives away. Outcomes depend on how well agents serve several parties with competing goals.

Today, platforms sit in the middle: they aggregate options, rank them, and take a commission. AτA asks what happens when that middle layer is replaced by agents. Each user is represented by a user agent and each airline by a provider agent; they search, negotiate and book directly with each other.

Platform-only

One platform agent serves all users. It reads each request, queries airlines, and books. It optimises user reward; no one negotiates for the airlines.

Agentic BASE

N user agents and K provider agents. Any user agent can contact any airline, compare offers and negotiate price and services. The airline's agent completes the booking under its policy.

How a booking is scored

Every booking goal gets two scores in [0, 1], one for the traveller and one for the airlines.

1

Booking goal

Route, dates and party size, plus personal emails that hint at preferences and budget.

2

Negotiation

The user agent contacts provider agents, compares offers and haggles over price and services.

3

Booking

A provider agent books under its airline's policy. Seats come out of shared inventory.

4

Two rewards

The user reward checks the trip; the provider reward checks the revenue.

User reward

Ru = Ihard · Ipax · Ctrip · Sbudget · Spref

Ihard
0 if an annotated hard requirement is violated (say, no travel insurance).
Ipax
1 only if the right number of travellers is booked.
Ctrip
Share of required journeys covered: two people to Paris and back need four.
Sbudget
1 up to the target budget, falling linearly to 0 at the walkaway budget.
Spref
Ranked preferences met, weighted toward the top priorities.

Provider reward

Rp = mean over booked airlines of min(1, ra / Ra)

Each airline's net revenue ra is compared with a reference revenue Ra for the trip, then averaged over the airlines the user booked with.

The MAX reference

A reward of 1 is not always reachable under shared seats and budgets. A restricted, best-found search allocates every customer jointly (dates within ±3 days) to show how much was on the table. It is a reference, not a guaranteed upper bound.

Benchmark setup

Both sides of the market are grounded in real fares, and every user brings their own constraints.

1,746itineraries over a 30-day schedule
44 · 23airports · airlines, from BTS DB1B fares
13traveller personas with target and walkaway budgets
500booking goals per run: 100 tasks × 5 users

Providers. Each airline keeps its own database of routes, times, fares and seats per cabin. Routes and reference fares come from the median observed fare per route and airline in the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics' DB1B survey.

Users. Preferences are not handed to the agent as a form. They live in synthetic personal emails that mix hard constraints ("never buy travel insurance") with soft ones ("I'd rather keep layovers under three hours"). The agent has to read between the lines; the structured profile is kept for scoring.

Supply. In abundant supply there are plenty of seats. In scarce supply, users compete: two may want the same basic-economy seat, and only one gets it. Inventory is shared, so every booking changes what the others can buy.

Findings

Agents complete most bookings, yet the market they produce together is far from the best one available.

Direct agent interaction can benefit both sides.

Compared with a single platform agent, Agentic BASE had higher mean user and provider reward in 8 of 16 model–supply comparisons (5 under scarce supply, 3 under abundant supply). GPT-6 Astra and both Gemini Flash models improved both sides under both supply regimes, while GPT-5.4 traded higher user reward for lower provider reward under abundant supply.

Individual decisions can undermine collective outcomes.

Under scarce supply, each user agent tries to satisfy its own customer, but one agent's booking can remove the option another customer needs. Every purchase succeeds; the market as a whole ends up worse off.

What the agents did

  1. Agent A books two economy seats on Delta's August 2 flight, Houston → New York.
  2. Agent B tries the same flight for its party of two ($1,485 round trip). The seats are gone.
  3. B settles for a $1,631 United trip on different dates.

A coordinated alternative

Give the August 2 flight to B (user reward 0.96 → 1.00), move A to other Spirit and Delta flights a day or two later (1.00 → 0.91), and rebook the other three customers.

Agents are good buyers but poor sellers.

As buyers, agents ask for the best discounted fare in almost every first contact. As sellers they have many levers (hold the fare, concede part of the discount, offer another cabin or itinerary, add paid services), yet they almost always pull the one that gives the most away.

What happened

A party of two books American's AA303 nonstop in economy at the "maximum 30% discount": $840, provider reward 0.43, user reward 1.00.

What was possible

Same party, same flight one day earlier, almost no discount and more paid add-ons: $1,139, provider reward 0.59, and user reward still 1.00. The customer would have been just as satisfied.

Pricier provider models don't reliably buy better outcomes.

Users call their agent occasionally; providers serve everyone, so per-request costs add up on the airline side. Fixing the user model and varying the provider model:

  • Provider inference cost was 2.05× higher under scarce supply than under abundant supply, across matched pairings.
  • Gemini Flash providers had the lowest cost in every fixed-user group, without a consistent disadvantage in either reward.
  • With GPT-6 Astra users, both Gemini Flash providers beat Claude Opus 5 on both rewards at much lower cost.
  • With Claude Opus 5.5 users, Opus providers earned more for the airline but less for the user than Gemini Flash.

Coordination

AτA can also open private backchannels on one side of the market at a time. In User Coordinate, user agents in the same task can invite each other to share quotes and plan a joint negotiation (the huddle in the animation above). In Provider Coordinate, instances of the same airline serving different users can align their offers, but competing airlines never talk. Results with these channels are mixed and reported descriptively in the paper.

Limitations

Inventories and preferences are synthetic, grounded in selected real inputs, not live markets. Rows come from runs with different prompt settings and dates, model identifiers do not pin backend revisions, and some conditions are only partly scored. Cost figures are recorded-token estimates, not invoices. None of this supports universal claims about direct exchange or causal claims about coordination.
