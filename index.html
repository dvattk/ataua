Users compete for limited seats

There are fewer seats than users want, so user agents compete for the same flights. Each dot is one model on both sides. The dashed MAX line is the best allocation found for each market.

Scarce supply

No model is Pareto optimal

Every model lands well inside the MAX line, so a better outcome was available. With users as happy as under GPT-6 Astra, providers could have earned 0.63 instead of 0.34.

Abundant supply

Enough seats to go around

There are plenty of seats, so user agents compete less for the same flights. Users do well: every model reaches 0.78 user reward or more, and GPT-6 Astra hits 0.97.

Abundant supply

Still not Pareto optimal

Every model still lands inside the MAX line. With users as happy as under GPT-6 Astra, providers could have earned 0.46 instead of 0.38; for every other model they miss at least 0.35.

Why the gap?

Every agent wins its own race

Under scarce supply, each user agent books the best trip for its own customer. Agent A takes the last two economy seats on Delta's August 2 flight. Agent B wanted the same flight for its own party of two, found it sold out, and paid $1,631 for a United trip on other dates.

A coordinated allocation gives B the Delta flight and moves A to flights a day or two later. A gives up a little; the market gains a lot.

Good buyers, poor sellers

Airline agents can hold the fare, concede part of the 30% discount, offer another cabin, or add paid services. Instead they reach for one lever: the full discount, usually in the first quote. Every booking got the maximum 30% off (858 under scarce supply, 531 under abundant supply), while customers spent a median of only 52–55% of their budgets.
